Harut and Marut in the Quran and Hadith

The Qur’anic account, principal narration families, disputed hadith gradings, and the boundaries between scripture and later storytelling.

Harut and Marut are named together in Qur’an 2:102. The detailed story of their descent, temptation and punishment has a disputed status in Sunni hadith scholarship. For a chapter that draws on Islamic sources, keep the Qur’anic account distinct from later reports, and retain the disagreement where a narration is disputed. This reference covers the direct Qur’anic material, the principal report families, their major authenticity assessments, and the limits relevant to fiction. It is not an exhaustive inventory of every manuscript variant or chain.

What the Quran directly says

Their names occur in Al Baqarah 2:102, the only verse that names either of them. Under the standard reading and the widely held interpretation identifying them as the two angels, the verse establishes:

  • The names Harut and Marut, and the location Babil, usually rendered Babylon.

  • Something was sent down to the two angels there.

  • Before teaching anyone, both gave a warning.

  • People learned from them something used to separate a man and his wife.

  • Such harm could happen only by Allah’s permission.

  • The learners acquired what harmed them and did not benefit them.

  • The verse condemns exchanging oneself for this, including the loss of a share in the Hereafter.

  • In the surrounding account, Sulayman is cleared of disbelief, while the devils are condemned for disbelief and teaching magic.

Qur’an 2:102 with Arabic and translation

Their warning is:

إِنَّمَا نَحْنُ فِتْنَةٌ فَلَا تَكْفُرْ

Meaning: We are only a test, so do not disbelieve. Here, fitnah means a trial or test. Al Saʿdi explains the angels’ teaching as a test accompanied by explicit warning, whereas the devils teach to deceive and mislead. He understands Allah’s permission as permission within His decree, not approval of the act. This is tafsir explaining the verse, rather than another quotation from the Prophet ﷺ. Al Saʿdi on 2:102

The main report attributed to the Prophet

Musnad Ahmad 6178, also transmitted in Sahih Ibn Hibban 6186, contains the principal extended report through Ibn Umar. In this version, angels contrast their obedience with human wrongdoing. Harut and Marut are chosen and sent down. A beautiful woman associated with al Zuhrah tests them. They initially refuse demands involving shirk and killing a child, but drink wine; while intoxicated, they have intercourse with her and kill the child. They are then offered punishment in this world or the Hereafter and choose this world.

These are the contents of a transmitted report, not additional details stated in the Qur’an. Its presence in Musnad Ahmad does not establish authenticity by itself. Report and Shuʿayb al Arnaʾut’s assessment

Scholar or source Assessment Implication
Ibn Hajar Describes the Ibn Umar narration’s chain as hasan in Fath al Bari, 10/235 in the cited edition. He considers the collected routes evidence that the story has a basis. Some respected scholars accepted a basis for the extended narrative.
Ibn Kathir Concludes that the detailed stories lack a sound, connected prophetic report, and traces the stronger transmission through Ibn Umar to Kaʿb al Ahbar. On his assessment, the expanded account should not be asserted as established prophetic teaching.
Shuʿayb al Arnaʾut Judges Ahmad 6178’s chain weak and its text invalid. He rejects that version as authenticated prophetic narration.
Al Albani Judges the principal account invalid as a prophetic attribution in al Silsilah al Daʿifah 170; also discusses related routes separately. His rejection must be attributed to him, rather than presented as universal agreement.

Sources: Ibn Hajar’s grading, Ibn Kathir’s discussion, al Arnaʾut on Ahmad 6178, al Albani’s assessments in the thematic collection. Hadith numbers and volume pagination can differ between editions.

The report through Kab al Ahbar

A significant transmission runs Ibn Umar → Kaʿb al Ahbar, rather than Ibn Umar → the Prophet ﷺ. It describes angels being selected, receiving prohibitions and then violating them. Al Arnaʾut judges this chain sound and stronger than the version attributed to the Prophet. A sound chain to Kaʿb establishes the attribution to Kaʿb; it does not turn his account into a saying of the Prophet or prove every event it describes. The Kaʿb transmission and grading

Ibn Kathir treats the expanded material as deriving from Israʾiliyyat, reports from earlier scriptural traditions. That designation concerns provenance; it does not mean that every individual report is automatically proven false. Ibn Kathir on 2:102

The account narrated by Aisha

A separate report says that, shortly after the Prophet’s death, a woman from Dumat al Jandal came to Aisha seeking help after becoming involved in magic. The woman claimed that an older woman took her to Babylon, each riding a black dog. There she encountered two men hanging by their feet. They warned her against disbelief and urged her to return. She persisted, underwent an act at an oven, and described an armored rider leaving her, which the two identified as her faith. She subsequently claimed that grain grew and became bread at her commands, and expressed remorse.

Ibn Kathir judges the chain good to Aisha while calling the account strange. The crucial distinction is that Aisha reports what this woman told her. It is not a statement by the Prophet, and the episode occurs after his death. The woman’s account therefore cannot be presented as prophetic confirmation that the angels remain suspended and accessible. Full report and Ibn Kathir’s grading

Other stories and their status

Detail encountered in retellings Source status
A woman named al Zuhrah, with Persian or other names in different versions Appears in expanded reports; the Qur’an names no woman.
She learns a name or formula enabling ascent to heaven, then becomes Venus A separate expanded narrative motif; not stated in the Qur’an and rejected by a number of commentators.
The angels serve as judges and rule unjustly because of attraction to her Appears in some versions, not as a direct Qur’anic statement.
They seek intercession from Idris, or their story is dated to his era Reported variants, not an established Qur’anic date or an undisputed prophetic account.
A choice between worldly punishment and punishment in the Hereafter Part of the disputed extended narrative.
Suspension upside down in a Babylonian well, iron restraints, inaccessible water or punishment until Judgment Details vary between reports; these cannot all be authenticated together by accepting one chain.

Al Qurtubi reproduces and rejects the expanded temptation story. The variants themselves must not be combined into a single allegedly authenticated biography. Al Qurtubi on 2:102, additional reports and assessments

Two other purported prophetic sayings need separate treatment:

  • A curse on al Zuhrah for tempting the angels: al Albani grades the report through Ali mawduʿ, fabricated, in al Silsilah al Daʿifah 913. Report and grading

  • A warning that the world is more enchanting than Harut and Marut: transmitted in different forms and routes. The Ibn Busr wording is graded weak by al Albani; the Abu al Darda wording receives stronger rejection, including fabricated or munkar assessments in his works. These are not dependable additions to the authenticated Sunnah. Ibn Busr wording, other wording and assessments

Differences within tafsir

The standard recitation reads al malakayn, the two angels. A reported irregular reading, al malikayn, means two kings. Classical commentators also debate whether ma in the clause about what was sent down is relative, meaning what, or negative, meaning not. These grammatical approaches produce different identifications and explanations. Al Qurtubi favors a negative construction; al Saʿdi follows the interpretation identifying Harut and Marut as angels who teach under warning as a test. The majority explanation should not be described as the only interpretation ever proposed. Al Qurtubi, al Saʿdi

Ibn Baz summarizes the well known scholarly view as two angels sent down who taught while warning people against learning the magic. Ibn Baz

Related authentic texts

These texts establish relevant background, but none supplies a biography of Harut and Marut:

  • Sahih al Bukhari 2766: the Prophet includes sihr among the seven destructive sins. Hadith

  • Sahih Muslim 2813b: Iblis sends out his agents and particularly praises an agent who separates a man and his wife. This parallels the marital separation in the verse without naming either angel. Hadith

  • Sahih Muslim 2996: angels were created from light, while jinn were created from fire. This is a general statement about their creation, not a description of these two angels’ appearance. Hadith

  • Qur’an 21:26–27: angels are honored servants who act by Allah’s command. 66:6 specifically describes the angels guarding the Fire as obeying His commands. Commentators invoke these texts in discussing the temptation story. 21:26–27, 66:6

Boundaries for the upcoming chapter

Use the Qur’anic foundation directly: the two names, Babylon, instruction preceded by warning, a test involving disbelief, marital separation, and harm remaining subject to Allah’s decree. The verse condemns the learners’ choice; it does not describe the angels as having disbelieved. The widely held explanation of their commission also does not make their teaching an endorsement of magic. Qur’an 2:102, Ibn Baz’s explanation

Keep additional narrative choices labeled as disputed reports or fiction: a present day prison, a surviving well, visits by later sorcerers, their feelings or personal histories, the details of punishment, and any claim that they invented all magic. Neither the verse nor an undisputed prophetic biography fixes these elements. Their appearance, ages, number of wings, exact date, present whereabouts and eventual fate are not established by the direct passage. The surrounding reference to Sulayman does not itself supply a precise date for their descent.

For a chapter built strictly on established revelation, leave the temptation and punishment story out of the narrator’s asserted history. If the chapter uses a transmitted legend, identify which version and scholarly assessment it follows. If a well, imprisonment or later encounter becomes part of the novel, record it as the novel’s chosen lore rather than silently calling it a Qur’anic fact.
